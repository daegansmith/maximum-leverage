Findings · Fishers AI workshop · October 22 to November 3, 2026

What Moved The Room

For two weeks we rebuilt one presentation and its offer, and tested almost every change in simulated rooms of local business owners before keeping it. Here's what actually changed how many of them bought, what didn't, and what the tests taught us about testing.

The presentation. A free evening AI workshop for local business owners in Fishers, Indiana. Jimmy presents. It teaches through eight beliefs and ends in a three-tier offer. Buying happens at the back of the room that night.

The tests. Rooms of simulated owners (roofers, salons, pizza shops, dentists, accountants, food trucks, a few skeptics, a few burned by agencies), each with a business, a temperament and a money situation. Rooms of 24, 50, 72, 100 and 120. Each owner read the whole night, bought or didn't, and said why. Along the way: price tests, skeptic studies, burned-owner studies and cold readers grading the offer.

The catch. The rooms buy more readily than real ones, so read the gap between versions, never the level. And the same version, read twice, swings by about 10%: 2 to 5 buyers out of 50, or $10,000 out of $90,000. These are predictions. The live room on November 9 is the judge.

The Short Version

  • Biggest lift: transparency for people who've been burned. One slide on how it works and a promise of the whole bill added about 17 buyers per 100 across two rounds.
  • The door price sets how many walk through. $997 / $2,997 / $4,997 brought in 43 buyers per 100. The earlier $1,997 / $3,997 / $7,497 brought in 24.
  • His words beat the cleaner rewrite, once you test the whole night. Read alone, the rewrite won every grade. Run as the whole night, his words sold 19% more.
  • A number they believe beats a bigger number. Owners doubting Jim's $673,000 went from 17 to 5 when it became a P.S.
  • Anything that makes the cheapest tier feel complete pulls buyers down to it. It happened four separate times.
  • Some walls never move. "I have to ask my wife" held at 16 to 21 per 100 through every price and every version.

How The Presentation Evolved

The Good price decides how many come through the door ("two grand is my rent"). The build prices decide how many builds you'd owe against a cap of 11. "I have to ask my wife" topped the reasons for waiting at every price. Changed: prices set at $997, $2,997 and $4,997.

2. Whose Words · October 24 and 25

Two offers. A cleaner rewrite (B), and the October 9 offer in his own words. Read alone by three cold readers, B won every grade, and every reader bought one tier higher. He called it unfair: B carried every structural fix and his version didn't. So we built A2: his words on B's skeleton, with the same fixes. Then we tested the whole night, presentation into offer, in a room of 100.

3. The Burned And The Skeptical · October 25

We pulled the 28 hardest owners (10 skeptics, 18 burned by an agency) and asked what stopped them. The walls: "Show me it gets built before you show me a price" (12). "One client isn't proof" (8). "Another monthly bill" (7). And one the presentation itself created: Jimmy's story said he once "promised more websites than he could build," and then the offer sold websites.

Two tests that day were dropped. A "see it in your business first" picture added Good buyers (28 to 36) but cost Better ones, and he cut it because it "made Better buyers cautious" and pulled in weaker customers. A6.3, which made the meeting sound more finished, sold the same as A6 (57 against 56) and moved two Better buyers down to Good. He kept A6: "we're really splitting hairs."

4. The Numbers · October 27

A new room of 24 read the old night and the newly locked one side by side. The new one sold 13 against 10. The biggest loss in both: values that read as made up. Jim's bread company, losing $4.58 a box, was shown making $673,000 a year. That was the main target.

So Belief 5 was rebuilt from the bottom: the math per box, $140 a box as a marketing budget against the other guy's $7, then "say it's only 30%, still six times the other guy." The $673,000 dropped to a P.S. The offer's values came into range at the same time ($35,640 total, the story at $1,997 against a video company's $5,000).

The same morning, two more tests went nowhere. A slide for "not my kind of business" owners ("Not A Bread Business? Neither Were These," eight real clients) moved none of the four owners it was built for: three had already checked out during Jim's bread numbers, before the slide arrived. And repeating "this offer lives here, in this room" cost two owners who won't spend without their spouse. Changed: that line came out of the written offer, to be said live. Across five rooms (120 owners) the two finalist nights tied: 65 buyers each.

Adding the money dashboard to the cheapest tier also tested even (66 against 65). It gave Good buyers another reason, not new buyers, and Good can't actually deliver it. Not adopted.

What Made The Difference

Moved it up · the most

1. Answer The Burned Owner Before They Ask

The two biggest gains of the whole build came from the same place: the owners who'd paid for promises before. "How This Works" (+9 buyers, about +$35,000 per 100), then the whole bill and the named people (+8 buyers, about +$16,000). Neither one added a deliverable. They took the fear out of saying yes.

Before the stack, show how it works, what it costs in total, and who does it. Transparency sells to the people who've been burned, and it costs nothing with everyone else.

Moved it up

2. The Door Price Sets The Room

Dropping the entry tier from $1,997 to $997 and the top from $7,497 to $4,997 nearly doubled buyers (24 to 43 per 100). The top tiers set how much building you sign up for. The bottom tier sets how many people start at all.

Price the first step for the owner whose rent it would be. Make money on the climb, not the door.

Moved it up

3. Test The Night, Not The Page

Read alone, the polished rewrite beat his words with every reader. Run inside the whole night, his words sold 41 against 32. A grader judges an offer's logic. A buyer reacts to what the night has already made them believe, and to lines like "our consultations are $1,997 each."

Never pick an offer by reading it alone. Run it at the end of the presentation it lives in.

Moved it up

4. A Number They Believe Beats A Bigger Number

The $673,000 went from the most doubted number in the room to a P.S. most owners bought past. The per-box math they could check in their heads did the selling.

Lead with the number they can check. Let the big one ride underneath.

Moved it up

5. Prove The Marketer's Eye, Then Show It At Work

Two outside studies first (one option, 10 of 100 buy; a choice, 66 of 100; a third option, 43% more spent), then "So now you can see what I saw that Jamon didn't." Buyers rated "I need someone with a marketer's eyes, not just the tool" 4.3 out of 5. Non-buyers, 2.9.

The sale lives in the gap between the tool and the eye. Borrow outside proof that the eye is real, then show yours at work on a real client.

Moved it down · four times

6. Make The Cheapest Tier Feel Complete, And Buyers Step Down To It

A meeting stripped of everything it previewed. A "see it in your business first" picture. A meeting described as "ready," with the new site "already picked." The dashboard put into Good. Each time, Better buyers stepped down or Good buyers just got another reason. "My new site's already picked and ready." "I'll build it myself."

Good should be valuable and clearly the start. When it sounds like the finish, it eats the tier above it.

Moved it down

7. Repeated Scarcity Costs The People Who Need Their Spouse

"This offer lives here, in this room," said twice, stopped two owners who never decide without their partner: "I didn't love being told it disappears tonight." A free 24-hour hold would have won them back on paper (about 20 buyers instead of 16), but only if someone calls every hold the next morning. He kept "tonight," said once, live, and no hold: a pile of holds in the real world becomes phone chasing.

Say the deadline once and mean it. Repeating it doesn't add buyers. It only costs the careful ones.

Didn't reach them

8. Fix Doubt Where It Starts, Not After

Twice we tried to bring back owners who felt "that's bread, not my kind of business." A slide of eight real client businesses afterward moved none of them; they'd checked out during Jim's numbers. Five "for you" translation slides inside Jim's numbers cost 3 buyers. The one thing that worked was a single line on the total: "For you, that's every order. Every job."

A fix placed after the moment of doubt never reaches the person. One line in the right spot beats five slides.

Didn't move sales

9. Clarity Is The Floor, Not The Lever

Rebuilding every section as a clean lesson read easier and sold the same or less. A full sweep of every broken hand-off cut the number of lost owners and didn't move sales. Telling the truth ("about a day" instead of "five minutes") cost nothing either.

Fix clarity and honesty because a confused or misled room can't buy. Don't expect them to sell for you.

What Never Moved

  • "I have to ask my wife." 16 to 21 per 100 in every room, at every price, in every version. It's a follow-up problem, not a wording problem.
  • Cash-tight owners at the price. Most no's happen at the offer, and most of those are about cash, not doubt.
  • Three or four committed skeptics per 100. Never buyers, whatever we said.
  • "Jim's numbers are estimates." The only fix is a real result, not more slides.
  • "$14 a day." Helps a couple of buyers, puts off an analytical one. A wash every time.

What The Tests Taught Us About Testing

  • Read what they did, not what they felt. Owners grumbled about the $10,000 meeting and the $140 a box, and bought anyway. Fix only what stopped the no-buyers.
  • A test can reward the wrong thing. In the first overhaul, a reviewer stripped the meeting of everything it previewed, and the cold readers graded it higher, because an empty first tier pushes people to the next one. It took Daegan reading the copy to see the meeting had been gutted: "a meeting that doesn't hold any value." Since then, the simulation reads the slides exactly as spoken and asks first whether a person would buy at all.
  • Compare like with like. A version with every structural fix will beat one without them. That tells you about the fixes, not the words.
  • Know your noise, and know when to stop. The same version swings about 10% between reads. Once changes stop moving the number beyond that, the next lesson comes from a live room, not another pass on the deck.
  • Test the change, not the comment. Half of what we tried came from something a simulated owner said. Some of it helped, some of it hurt. A change stays only if the sales hold it up.
