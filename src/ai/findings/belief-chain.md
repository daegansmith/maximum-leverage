# I Cut My Belief Chain To Three Steps. It Sold The Same.

!! **Update, November 13.** The next morning I ran this test through Rich Schefren's Hidden Obstacles process. The big line below did not survive it.

!! The 7 extra buyers came from a test that had planted its own answers. Run again a cleaner way, on 100 simulated owners, answering the doubts before the ask did not beat saying nothing.

!! What still stands: the short chain didn't lose buyers. What I don't know yet: what belongs right before the ask. A live night settles that.

!! The story below is what I believed on November 12. I'm leaving it up. The record of the check is right under it.

October 19. I write myself a note.

I've built belief chains for years.

A chain is the road you walk a stranger down. Each belief sets up the next one, until buying feels like their own idea.

I never put a limit on the steps.

Twelve was fine.

[[1]]

Then I ran into a rule from Perry Belcher.

No more than three beliefs. Go past three, and the offer fails.

[[2]]

Put the two together and something clicked.

If every belief a person needs can be squeezed into three, the road gets shorter. And the conclusion lands harder.

That was the whole note. At the bottom I wrote what I'd test, and then two words.

"Nothing yet."

It stayed that way for 24 days.

On November 4, planning the Fishers registration page, I said it out loud: "I really want to focus on the three biggest beliefs that would move the needle."

But I couldn't test it in the real world.

So I said this instead:

"If I can't test it in the real world, I can at least do a simulation. And if the logic plays out for me, then yeah, let's go ahead and start working that way."

So we built the room. A simulation, NOT live sales. I'll say that again at the end.

100 local business owners. The same 100 every time.

Three versions of the same road. The long one. The short one. And the short one with answers to the doubts, right before the ask.

25 AI readers, each playing 20 owners, each seeing only one version. At every belief they could say yes, partly, or no.

[[3]]

The first answer came back fast.

Claude had scored it by rules. Short chain: 31 buyers. Long chain: 14.

Doubled.

But that score treated every belief as a door somebody walks out of. People don't read like that. So we threw it out and ran the readers blind.

[[4]]

**Long chain: 29 bought. Short chain: 28.**

**A tie.**

So here's what they actually did. They didn't leave at each belief. They said "partly" and kept walking.

325 times in the long chain. 116 in the short.

And all of that doubt rode along with them to the price.

[[5]]

Then we answered three questions right before the ask. The three someone always asks.

Isn't this just a fad?

My business is fine. What would this even fix?

Is it worth it for a business my size?

**35 bought.**

[[6]]

And that's the lesson.

**A shorter road doesn't sell more. Answering the doubts people CARRY to the price does.**

So I changed the skill.

It used to build one long chain and lock it. Now it builds the long chain, squeezes it one move at a time, and writes an answer for every belief it cuts. Then readers walk both versions blind before anything gets locked.

**Three is the goal. Never forced.**

[[skill]]

Now, the honest part.

One run. A prediction, not a result. Runs on a room like this swing by about four buyers, so the gain from the answers is just past that. The short-versus-long difference is well inside it.

**And the thing readers named most at the price wasn't in either chain. Proof from a business like theirs.**

So that's a belief I hadn't built at all.

But remember this.

**A shorter road doesn't sell more. Answering the doubts people carry to the price does.**

Mine was twelve steps.

What's yours?

**Before you cut one, ask the ones who got all the way to the price what was still in their head. Then answer that first.**

## What The Check Found The Next Morning

November 13. The test went through the Hidden Obstacles process: what are we pretending not to notice, what would we have to believe for this to make sense, then dispute each belief three ways.

**What we were pretending not to notice.** The result agreed with what I hoped. It went into a skill and onto this page the same night. Nobody had checked what it stood on.

| What the test assumed | What disputed it |
| --- | --- |
| Belcher's rule is three beliefs | My own notes on Belcher: more than three and the offer will most likely fail; keep each offer to one belief. Three is the ceiling. One is the target |
| Readers "carry the doubt forward" | The readers were handed that answer. Their choices were accept, reject, or "partly: carries a doubt forward" |
| The long chain creates more doubt, 325 against 116 | 325 over eight beliefs and 116 over three is about 40 a belief both times |
| We found what holds people back | The simulated owners were built with "cash tight," "pays a website guy" and "burned by an agency." Three of the four things reported were those |
| The answers caused the 7 extra buyers | That version added two answers, Jamon's proof story and more words, all at once |
| The test checked why I want a short chain | I want one because people run out of attention. Simulated readers don't get tired |

**The rerun.** Same short chain, four ways, 100 simulated owners, each one living all four versions. The only thing that changed was what got said right before the offer.

| Said before the offer | Paid, of 100 | Changed to paid | Changed away |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nothing | 6 | | |
| The two answers | 6 | 4 | 4 |
| Jamon's story alone | 8 | 7 | 5 |
| All three | 7 | 7 | 6 |

A tie. It can't be lined up against the 28 and 35 from the night before: different room, different instructions to the readers.

**Who paid was decided by how each owner was built.** All 8 owners tagged "impulsive" paid in at least one version. 1 of the 32 under $150K did. A past agency came up at the price 30 times, and 28 of those were owners built with "burned by an agency before."

**Leads, not findings.** Money was the thing most named at the price in every version. And Jamon's story told alone drew "he's a handyman, I'm not" from 21 of 92 non-buyers. With no story, nobody said it.

**The new way of looking.** A chain walk checks the logic of the chain and collects what people say at the price. It is not a sales test. What belongs right before the ask gets settled on a live night.

## How The Skill Runs After The Check

- **Three is the ceiling, one is the target.** Compress toward three, then ask which one the offer rests on.
- **No answer handed to the reader.** They write what the person thought. It gets coded after.
- **One change between versions.** Answers and a proof story are two versions, not one.
- **Nothing planted.** Every objection is reported with how many of the people who named it were built with it.
- **Rates, not piles.** Doubts are counted per belief.
- **The check runs first.** No finding from a walk goes into the skill or onto a page until it has been through the hidden-assumptions skill.
- **The trial changed.** "Three builds in a row" is gone. A simulated room can't settle whether the short chain sells better. A live night can.

## The Test

- **The room.** 100 local business owners, built from traits: business type, years in business, revenue, comfort with tech, age, attitude, plus whether they'd been burned by an agency, tried AI and given up, had cash tight, paid a website guy, were having a bad month, or assumed a pitch was coming.
- **The readers.** 25 independent AI agents, 20 owners each. Each agent saw one version only, so nobody compared.
- **At each belief.** Accept, partly (carry the doubt forward), or reject (stop).
- **At the ask.** Everyone who finished answered the same ask, the two doors (Build at $4,997 or 2 x $2,500, or the Meeting at $997), and said in a line what still held them back.
- **The three versions.** Registration chain: Jimmy's nine steps against two merged beliefs. Room chain: the deck's eight beliefs against a recap plus three beliefs. Then the short room chain with three answers placed after belief three and before the offer.
- **The answers.** "Isn't this just a fad?" (the phone in their hand that just did real work; about 1 in 7 shops has it working; websites in 2000). "My business is fine. What would this even fix?" (three counting questions out loud: unfollowed quotes, past customers not contacted in a year, calls to voicemail). "Is it worth it for a business my size?" (Jamon: handyman, $15,000 to $20,000 a month, now over $50,000, "AI by itself would have failed.")

## What Still Held People Back At The Ask

In every version, in this order of how often it was named:

1. Proof from a business like mine. Most named, and in neither chain.
2. Cash is tight, or the business makes under $100K.
3. "My website guy" or "I could build it myself."
4. Burned by an agency before.

Also found: the second belief in the short registration chain (free night, no pitch, one thing working on my phone) lost 23 readers. It carries too much. It's a candidate to split.

## How The Skill Ran Before

The version in my library on November 10, before this test.

- Mapped one long numbered chain from stranger to buyer. 28 steps was fine.
- Flagged the one to three gate beliefs everything hinges on.
- Mapped lead tiers onto the chain where needed.
- Locked the chain and handed it forward, so it wasn't re-derived every week.
- Nothing about shortening it. Nothing about testing it. Nothing about what happens to a belief that gets cut.

## How It Runs Now

The version from November 12.

- **One spine per stage.** Lead (stranger to raised hand), customer (lead to first purchase), backend (customer to the big yes). Each gets a compressed spine, ideally three beliefs, with the long chain kept beside it as the map.
- **The cycle.** Map it long. Sort every step into belief, experience or ask. Link test every pair with a bridge sentence in the prospect's voice. Compress one move at a time, each with its reason (merge, already concluded, order, floor). Check every surviving belief has a story, demo or proof strong enough to carry what was merged into it. Draft the objection block. Test with blind readers. Find the hidden beliefs. Add them. Repeat until no new one shows up above the noise.
- **The objection block.** Every cut belief, how it comes back in the prospect's words, and its answer. It sits right before the ask: beside the button on a page, after the last belief in a room, after discovery on a call.
- **Three is ideal, never forced.** A merge only counts if its bridge sentence writes cleanly on both sides and a fresh reader can say the merged belief back in plain words. Otherwise it's undone, and the skill reports the honest count: "Started at 12, ended at 5."
- **New refusals.** It won't compress without keeping the long chain and building the objection block. It won't report a chain walk scored by rules or by hand. It won't lock a stage that has no objection block before its ask.
- **On trial.** Long against short against short + answers, on every new chain, until short + answers matches or beats long three builds in a row. Then the long version gets dropped from the test. Build 1 is the Fishers room, November 12.

## What It Cost

About 3.3 million tokens for the blind run: 25 agents at roughly 130,000 each, most of it fixed start-up cost. The rule-based walk cost almost nothing and gave the wrong answer. Larger batches, 50 owners per agent, should about halve it, and the skill now says to do that.

## Words Used Here

- **Belief chain.** The numbered list of things a stranger has to believe, in order, to get from where they are to yes.
- **Spine.** The short version of the chain, ideally three beliefs, that the copy is built on.
- **Objection block.** The questions people still have right before the ask, each with its answer, standing in for the beliefs that got cut.
- **Blind readers.** AI readers who each see one version only and never see the others or the numbers.
- **Partly.** A reader's answer at a belief: it carries a doubt forward instead of stopping.
- **Hidden belief.** An objection people name at the ask that answers to no belief in the chain.
- **Chain walk.** The test: readers move through the chain belief by belief, then answer the ask.
